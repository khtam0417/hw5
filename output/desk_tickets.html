Reflection

Problem 10

I would evaluate this team by whether its decisions matched the database, whether it respected human approval, and whether its final reports accurately described what happened; a “Resolved” badge alone would not be enough. Ticket 101 showed both the strengths and the weaknesses of the system. Inventory correctly found that the size S Bulldog tee had zero stock and that invoice 501 blocked Bulldog Print Co from shipping. Accounting checked the available cash and prepared the $840 payment request, which I approved on the dashboard. Inventory then saved the reprint request, and Customer Service drafted the customer update. Those were useful contributions, but two of the ticket’s four runs were needed only because our completion rule was unclear, and the first customer draft became outdated after the payment. The Boss eventually caught that outdated message and requested a replacement, which was a good correction, but it also wrote notes over the tool’s 1,000-character limit twice, causing unnecessary retries. My Expected section predicted Boss → Inventory → Accounting, but the first run went Boss → Inventory and then Boss → Accounting directly; that flatter structure still achieved the purpose. I also expected Customer Service to draft after the reprint request; instead, it drafted before the payment and had to update the message later. Most importantly, my original plan ended at waiting_on_vendor, while the final outcome became “resolved for internal processing” after I clarified that sending messages was outside this simulation. That was a change in our definition of completion, not evidence that the tee arrived, and the Actual section correctly states that stock remains zero, the drafts are unsent, and the customer’s order is unfulfilled. Ticket 102 was the strongest performance, because its requirements and completion conditions were clearer. Facilities verified the $2,400 rent, the landlord, and the September 2 due date, then delegated to Accounting, which considered the pending $840 invoice when checking affordability. This matched my expected Boss → Facilities → Accounting flow. After I approved the rent payment, the lease’s next due date advanced to October 2. I expected the Boss to ask Facilities to verify the payment again, but it checked the paid request itself and resolved the ticket without another delegation, which saved work because the necessary evidence was already available. Ticket 103 handled uncertainty well but needed a clearer distinction between proposing an offer and settling the original request. Inventory confirmed that only eight of the 20 requested medium hoodies were available, and it did not assume that Bulldog supplied hoodies just because its listed specialty is “apparel reprint.” Accounting compared 10% and 15% discounts, and Customer Service drafted the eight-hoodie offer. The 10% option was $52.20 each, or $417.60 in total, with a $241.60 gross margin (about 57.9% of the proposed revenue); these figures matched my Expected section. However, I expected a needs_human status, while the Boss chose waiting_on_customer even though the message had not been sent. Accounting also did not perform the cash and pending-request checks I had predicted for evaluating restocking; it focused on pricing the eight available units, which was enough for the draft offer but did not establish that restocking was affordable. My approval of the discount did not mean the customer accepted it, and the ticket was ultimately resolved when I declined the original 20-hoodie request and kept the eight-hoodie proposal as an unsent alternative. The Cash tab gives the clearest overall check on the team: checking started at $3,400, fell to $2,560 after invoice 501, and ended at $160 after the rent. Ticket 103 did not change cash, and none of the later status updates moved money. The agents prepared requests, but only my approvals executed payments. For a shop this small, several parts would have been simpler with one agent using the same MCP tools. Ticket 102’s lease lookup, payment-history check, cash check, and approval request form a short sequence that one agent could handle without passing context through Facilities and Accounting, and ticket 103’s stock lookup and discount calculations could also happen in one conversation. Multiple agents made responsibilities visible on the dashboard, but they added repeated lookups, reports, and model calls: across ticket 101’s four runs alone, recorded usage exceeded 330,000 tokens. I would keep the separate approval mechanism and the audit trail regardless of the number of agents, because those protections come from the implementation rather than from having five roles. Three more problems this team could handle with its existing tools are a student group requesting a discount quote for 12 Yale Caps, a duplicate rent notice for the period already paid, and a customer asking whether Crest Mugs are available. For the caps, Inventory could check the recorded stock of 40, Accounting could use get_pricing and evaluate_discount, and Customer Service could save a proposal without claiming a sale. For the duplicate notice, Facilities and Accounting could compare get_lease_details with list_payments to identify the existing payment, and Customer Service could draft an explanation, all without paying again. For the mugs, get_inventory would show zero stock, and Customer Service could draft an honest availability response without promising a restock date. Three problems it could not fully solve are sourcing the missing 12 hoodies from a new supplier, processing a customer refund, and forecasting inventory demand for next semester. Sourcing would require verified supplier catalogs, product-to-vendor mappings, quotations, purchase-order submission, and delivery tracking; I would extend Inventory with a procurement role and keep purchase approval with a person. Refunds would require sales and return records, eligibility rules, and a payment-provider refund tool, coordinated by Accounting and Customer Service under human approval. Demand forecasting would require historical sales, seasonal information, and forecasting tools used by an analytics agent working with Inventory, since our current inventory snapshot and pricing table cannot support a reliable forecast. The main lesson for me is that clear completion rules, accurate shared records, and controlled actions matter more than the number of agents: this team was useful when it checked facts and divided relevant work, but it needed human direction when the task involved business choices or ambiguous definitions of “done.”
